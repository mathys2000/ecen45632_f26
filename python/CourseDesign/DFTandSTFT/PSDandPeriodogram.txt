How should I define power spectral density for a recorded discrete time signal of finite length

Claude Sonnet 5.5:

## Definition

For a wide-sense stationary random process x[n], the PSD is the DTFT of its autocorrelation:

$$S_x(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} r_x[k]\,e^{-j\omega k}, \qquad r_x[k]=E\{x[n+k]x^*[n]\}$$

You can't compute this from a recording, because it requires an expectation and infinite data. From a finite record of length L you can only **estimate** the PSD, so the practical definition is an estimator.

## The periodogram (single record)

With window w[r] and FFT length N ≥ L:

$$\hat S[k] = \frac{1}{f_s \sum_{r} w^2[r]}\left|\sum_{r=0}^{L-1} x[r]\,w[r]\,e^{-j2\pi kr/N}\right|^2 \quad \left[\tfrac{\text{signal units}^2}{\text{Hz}}\right]$$

The normalization is what makes it a PSD rather than a magnitude display:

- **Divide by Σw²** so the estimate accounts for the energy the window removes. With this choice, the PSD integrated over frequency recovers the signal's mean-square power: Σₖ Ŝ[k]·Δf ≈ (1/L) Σ|x w|² / (Σw²/L), which for white noise gives the correct variance.
- **Divide by f_s** to get units per Hz instead of per cycle/sample. (Without it you have power per rad/sample, scaled by f_s differently.)
- For a **real** signal, the one-sided PSD doubles the values at 0 < f < f_s/2 (not at DC or Nyquist) so the total power is preserved.

Equivalently, you can use the ENBW from the assignment: the power measured in one bin is Ŝ[k]·ENBW. SciPy's `signal.periodogram(x, fs, window="hann", scaling="density")` does exactly this.

## Why a single periodogram isn't enough

- **Bias and leakage:** the window convolves the true PSD with |W(f)|². Low sidelobes protect weak components next to strong ones, as in A2.
- **High variance:** a periodogram's variance doesn't shrink as L grows, because it stays roughly equal to Ŝ². Each bin is like a single chi-squared sample with 2 degrees of freedom.

The standard fix is **Welch's method**: split the record into overlapping windowed segments, compute each periodogram with the normalization above, and average them. This is the STFT you built, with |X_m[k]|² averaged over m. The cost is resolution, since the segments are shorter than the whole record. Overlap of 50% with Hann is a common choice. `signal.welch` does this.

## Choices to state when you report one

- Sample rate, window, segment length, overlap, FFT length, and one- or two-sided.
- **Detrending**, such as removing the mean, so DC doesn't leak into nearby bins.
- Whether you report **density** (units²/Hz) or **spectrum** (units² per bin, which suits sinusoids).

The distinction matters: a stationary noise floor should be read as density, and a sinusoid's peak height should be read as power, since a pure tone isn't a density at all (its true PSD is a delta function).

## Relation to the assignment

This is why the assignment says to label spectrogram displays "relative dB" unless you implement the proper normalization. The `|X|²` I plotted in `run_all.py` is normalized only to its own maximum, so it shows relative level, not calibrated PSD. To make it a PSD you'd scale |X_m[k]|² by 1/(f_s Σw²), double the one-sided bins, and, because a spectrogram is a single-frame estimate, remember each pixel still has the periodogram's high variance.

Welch’s method estimates the Power Spectral Density (PSD) of a signal by dividing the data into overlapping segments, applying a window function, computing individual periodograms, and averaging them to reduce noise and variance. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch%27s_method), [2] (https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.welch.html)
You can compute it directly in Python using SciPy Signal Welch or in MATLAB using MATLAB pwelch. [1] (https://www.mathworks.com/help/signal/ref/pwelch.html), [2] (https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.welch.html)

How Welch's Method Works

• Segmentation: Split the time-series signal into L shorter segments of length M. [1] (https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/5688766), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch%27s_method)
• Overlap: Allow adjacent segments to overlap (commonly by 50% or 90%) to retain data points lost by windowing. [1] (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6525419/), [2] (https://pub.towardsai.net/turning-sensor-noise-into-signal-fft-and-power-spectrum-features-66fbf0642cd1)
• Windowing: Multiply each segment by a taper window—such as a Hann or Hamming window—to minimize spectral leakage at the edges. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u6TzodpIeDQ), [2] (https://pub.towardsai.net/turning-sensor-noise-into-signal-fft-and-power-spectrum-features-66fbf0642cd1)
• Periodogram Calculation: Compute the squared magnitude of the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT/FFT) for each windowed segment. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch%27s_method), [2] (https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/5688766)
• Averaging: Average the resulting periodograms across all segments to lower the variance of the noise floor. [1] (https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.welch.html), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch%27s_method)

Key Parameters to Balance

• Segment Length (nperseg): Longer segments improve frequency resolution (ability to separate close frequencies) but decrease the number of available averages (higher variance). [1] (https://dsp.stackexchange.com/questions/81640/trying-to-understand-the-nperseg-effect-of-welch-method), [2] (https://pub.towardsai.net/turning-sensor-noise-into-signal-fft-and-power-spectrum-features-66fbf0642cd1)
• Overlap (noverlap): Higher overlap increases the smoothness and independence balance of the estimate, though it requires more computation. [1] (https://dsp.stackexchange.com/questions/81640/trying-to-understand-the-nperseg-effect-of-welch-method), [2] (https://pub.towardsai.net/turning-sensor-noise-into-signal-fft-and-power-spectrum-features-66fbf0642cd1)
• Window Type: The Hann window is the default and preferred choice for general-use data due to its leakage-suppression properties. [1] (https://sapienlabs.org/factors-that-impact-power-spectrum-density-estimation/), [2] (https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.welch.html)

To mathematically move from a single standard periodogram of an entire sequence to Welch's averaged periodogram of overlapping segments, you must break down the process into segmentation, window scaling, and averaging.
Here is the step-by-step mathematical formulation.

1. The Standard Periodogram (Whole Sequence)

For a full sequence \(x[n]\) of length \(N\), the standard periodogram estimate \(\hat{P}_{\text{per}}(f)\) is defined as:
\(\^{P}_{\text{per}}(f)=\frac{1}{N}\left|{}\sum _{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi fn}\right|{}^{2}\)

2. Segmenting and Windowing

Welch's method breaks the long sequence \(x[n]\) into \(K\) overlapping segments, each of length \(M\).
Let the \(i\)-th segment be \(x_i[n]\). If the shift (distance between the starts of adjacent segments) is \(D\), then:
\(x_{i}[n]=x[n+iD],\quad n=0,1,\dots ,M-1,\quad i=0,1,\dots ,K-1\)
(For example, for a 50% overlap, \(D = M/2\).)
Each segment is then multiplied by a window function \(w[n]\) of length \(M\). To ensure the spectral estimate is unbiased, we calculate a window normalization factor (\(U\)):
\(U=\frac{1}{M}\sum _{n=0}^{M-1}|{}w[n]|{}^{2}\)

3. The Modified Periodogram of a Segment

Instead of the full-sequence periodogram, we compute a modified periodogram \(P_i(f)\) for each individual windowed segment \(i\):
\(P_{i}(f)=\frac{1}{M\cdot U}\left|{}\sum _{n=0}^{M-1}x_{i}[n]w[n]e^{-j2\pi fn}\right|{}^{2}\)
Substituting the segmentation definition into this yields the direct link to your original sequence:
\(P_{i}(f)=\frac{1}{M\cdot U}\left|{}\sum _{n=0}^{M-1}x[n+iD]w[n]e^{-j2\pi fn}\right|{}^{2}\)

4. The Final Welch PSD Formula

The final Welch power spectral density estimate \(\hat{P}_{\text{Welch}}(f)\) is the arithmetic average of these individual modified periodograms:
\(\^{P}_{\text{Welch}}(f)=\frac{1}{K}\sum _{i=0}^{K-1}P_{i}(f)\)
\(\^{P}_{\text{Welch}}(f)=\frac{1}{K\cdot M\cdot U}\sum _{i=0}^{K-1}\left|{}\sum _{n=0}^{M-1}x[n+iD]w[n]e^{-j2\pi fn}\right|{}^{2}\)

Summary of the Transformation

• The single large summation over \(N\) is replaced by a double operation: an inner Fourier transform over a shorter length \(M\), and an outer summation averaging over \(K\) blocks.
• The scaling factor changes from \(\frac{1}{N}\) to \(\frac{1}{K\cdot M\cdot U}\) to properly normalize for the window's energy loss and the number of averaged segments.
